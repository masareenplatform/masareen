Abstract
MASAREN ( ‫ ) ﺭ‬is an Impact Infrastructure as a Service (IIaaS) platform
that converts verified real-world actions into traceable records, measurable outcomes, and
governable digital assets. The architecture combines domain-oriented services, event-driven
integration, geospatial evidence, human review, and cloud-hosted machine intelligence. Its
purpose is not merely to operate software at scale; it is to preserve the chain of custody
between an intended activity, the evidence that an activity occurred, the decision that
validates it, and the economic or reputational value assigned to the result.
This document provides the comprehensive technical architecture, implementation patterns, and operational guidelines for the MASAREN platform. It serves as the definitive
reference for technical implementation, deployment strategies, and system integration.
Blueprint scope. This document aligns product, engineering, data, security, operations, finance, compliance, and partner-integration teams around one target architecture.
It defines bounded contexts, authoritative data ownership, event contracts, trust boundaries, deployment topology, reliability targets, control evidence, and decision rights. The
differentiating design is a verification-first operating model delivered consistently through
React/TypeScript and Flutter clients, supported by durable event streams, policy-enforced
access, independently deployable services, and a combined backend used only as a development convenience rather than as the production topology.

1 Introduction
Mission and Purpose MASAREN stands as a verification-centered Impact Infrastructure
Platform that transforms real-world field activity into digitally verifiable and economically
interpretable records. The platform addresses a recurring market problem: sponsors, organizations, field participants, auditors, and beneficiaries often hold different fragments of evidence
and use different definitions of success. MASAREN provides a shared chain of custody from
intention to execution, from field evidence to a verification decision, and from that decision to
a reportable impact asset. The platform therefore connects planned outcomes with field-level
actions, physical evidence with governed digital records, and demonstrated performance with
measurable reputation.
Platform Vision Verification-Centric Architecture. Every asset creation requires multilayered verification through AI, GPS, and human-in-the-loop review. Cross-Platform Consistency. Web and mobile experiences preserve parity for core journeys while adapting interaction patterns to each channel through shared backend contracts. Event-Driven Data
Consistency. Transactional systems remain authoritative for current aggregate state, while
Kafka provides the durable integration history and distribution backbone. Event envelopes
conform to a common contract inspired by CloudEvents so that producers and consumers share
identifiers, timestamps, types, subjects, versions, and trace context (Apache Software Foundation, 2026; Cloud Native Computing Foundation, 2024). Cloud-First AI Strategy. No
production inference runs on application nodes; approved cloud providers are selected through
security, privacy, quality, residency, availability, and cost evaluation. Enterprise-Grade Security. Every access request is evaluated using identity, device, workload, resource, and policy
context rather than trusted solely because it originates inside a network boundary. This follows
the resource-centered principles of zero-trust architecture (National Institute of Standards and
Technology, 2020). Developer-First Design. The shared kernel contains only stable crosscutting primitives, while business models remain owned by bounded contexts. The combined
backend shortens local feedback cycles, but production releases preserve independent ownership,
scaling, and rollback.
Core Architecture Principles The MASAREN platform is built on the following engineering
principles. These principles are decision constraints: an implementation that violates them
requires a recorded architecture decision, named owner, expiry or review date, and migration
plan.
Domain-Driven Design: Complex business logic is organized into bounded contexts with
ubiquitous language, aggregate patterns, and domain events. Clean/Hexagonal Architecture: Separation of concerns places core business logic at the center, surrounded by infrastructure adapters. CQRS where it reduces coupling: Commands and queries may use separate
models when read and write concerns differ materially. Event sourcing is reserved for aggregates
that require full state reconstruction; it is not a blanket requirement for every service. Transactional Outbox Pattern: Aggregate changes and publication intent commit atomically in one
database transaction; a retryable relay publishes the event afterward. Evolutionary service

3

boundaries: Services communicate through versioned APIs or events. A deployable boundary
is justified by ownership, scaling, security, reliability, or release independence rather than by
an arbitrary service count. Repository Pattern: Abstract data access with domain-specific
repositories ensures persistence ignorance. Specification Pattern: Query building and filtering for complex data retrieval across bounded contexts.
Document Authority and Team Alignment This blueprint is the normative technical reference for product planning, solution design, implementation, security review, data governance,
deployment, incident response, and partner onboarding. Product teams use it to connect market outcomes to capabilities; domain teams use it to identify ownership and contracts; platform
teams use it to define paved roads and runtime controls; security and privacy teams use it to
map threats and evidence; data teams use it to govern lineage and metric definitions; and operations teams use it to derive service-level objectives, runbooks, and recovery exercises. Source
code and deployed configuration remain the executable truth, while any material divergence
from this blueprint must be captured in an architecture decision record and reconciled during
the next review cycle.
Architecture decisions are approved by a small architecture council consisting of the chief
architect, product lead, platform lead, security lead, and the accountable domain owner. The
council owns cross-domain standards but does not centralize routine implementation choices.
Each bounded context has one accountable team, one named technical owner, one data steward, and one operational on-call rotation. Interfaces have explicit producers and consumers,
compatibility rules, deprecation dates, and measurable acceptance criteria.
Market and Product Outcomes The design addresses six market expectations that determine platform viability. Trust is supported through evidence provenance, explainable decisions, tamper-evident audit history, and separation of duties. Speed is supported through
guided mobile capture, asynchronous verification, and predictable partner APIs. Scale is supported through independently scalable workloads, partitioned event streams, and object storage
for large evidence. Enterprise readiness is supported through tenant isolation, identity federation, policy-based authorization, audit exports, retention controls, and regional deployment
options. Economic sustainability is supported through workload-level cost attribution and unit
economics, consistent with the FinOps emphasis on connecting technology spend to business
value (FinOps Foundation, 2026). Ecosystem growth is supported through stable contracts,
developer documentation, sandbox environments, and consent-aware data sharing.
Quality Attributes and Acceptance Targets Quality attributes are treated as testable
product requirements rather than aspirations. The initial targets below define the production launch gate and are refined per service through service-level indicators and error budgets,
following the iterative SLO method described by Sloss et al. (2018).
Availability. Tier-1 user journeys target 99.9% monthly availability, while lower tiers publish
explicit objectives. Acceptance requires synthetic journey checks, server-side success ratios, a
dependency-aware SLO dashboard, and an approved error-budget policy.

4

Latency. Read APIs target 300 ms p95 server latency and command APIs target 700 ms p95,
excluding declared asynchronous work. Acceptance requires production histograms segmented
by tenant, region, endpoint, and release.
Durability and recovery. Accepted evidence and ledger records permit no acknowledged data
loss, with a recovery point objective of five minutes or less. Tier-1 regional service recovery
targets 60 minutes, and individual stateless-service recovery targets 15 minutes. Acceptance
requires automated backups, object versioning, rollback tests, quarterly restoration exercises,
and recorded recovery results.
Security and privacy. Internet-facing applications use OWASP ASVS Level 2 as the baseline, with risk-selected Level 3 controls for high-value verification and administrative functions.
Collection, purpose, access, retention, export, deletion, and consent must be policy-enforceable
and observable. Acceptance requires threat models, automated control tests, penetration reports, dependency attestations, data inventories, retention tests, and privacy review (European
Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016; OWASP Foundation, 2025a).
Operability and cost eﬀiciency. Every production workload emits correlated logs, metrics,
and traces through a vendor-neutral telemetry pipeline, propagates the W3C trace context
across synchronous and asynchronous boundaries, and exposes service-level indicators through
Prometheus-compatible metrics and governed Grafana dashboards. Each tenant, product journey, and major workload has attributable infrastructure and AI cost. Acceptance requires OpenTelemetry conformance, actionable alerts, owned runbooks, cost-allocation metadata, budget
alerts, and quarterly unit-economics review (FinOps Foundation, 2026; Grafana Labs, 2026;
OpenTelemetry Authors, 2026; Prometheus Authors, 2026; World Wide Web Consortium,
2021).

Canonical Business Entities and Ownership The entity model provides a shared vocabulary for APIs, events, analytics, and governance. An entity name has one semantic definition
even when individual services maintain projections. The owning context is the only context
allowed to change the authoritative state; other contexts consume a versioned contract and
retain only the fields needed for their purpose.
Identity entities. The Identity context owns a natural person or service principal, organization membership, credentials, assurance level, consent, and lifecycle status. No other context
stores credentials or independently redefines identity state.
Execution entities. The Execution context owns the Organization as a tenant or delivery
institution; the Program and Outcome as a funded intervention with geography, eligibility,
indicators, controls, schedule, and budget; and the Task and Assignment as an authorized unit
of work linked to a participant and workflow.
Verification entities. The Verification context owns the Submission and Evidence record,
including captured media or sensor data, provenance, checksums, retention class, and chain of
custody. It also owns the Verification Case, which contains automated signals, reviewer actions,
policy version, confidence, exceptions, appeal history, and final disposition.
Value and market entities. The Reputation context owns the Impact Asset and Reputation Profile. An impact asset is a governed representation of a verified outcome with quantity,
methodology, ownership, validity period, and revocation state. A reputation profile is a timebounded, explainable aggregation and never replaces raw evidence or appeal rights. The Marketplace context owns Opportunities, Applications, and Matches, while the Economic context
owns ledger entries and settlements linked to contractual bases and verification cases.
Governance entities. Platform Governance owns the Audit Record as an append-only account of security-sensitive and business-critical decisions with actor, action, resource, reason,
time, tenant, policy, and trace identifiers. Data stewards maintain the canonical entity glossary
and approve semantic changes before contract release.

High-Level Architecture Layers The MASAREN architecture follows a layered design with
seven distinct layers:
Presentation Layer: React/TypeScript web applications and Flutter mobile app serving
as the user-facing interfaces. Experience Layer: API Gateway (BFF pattern) at port 8080
providing routing, JWT authentication, rate limiting, and aggregation. Business Layer: 35+
domain services organized into bounded contexts (Identity, Execution, Verification, Reputation,
Marketplace, etc.). AI Layer: Cloud-based AI agents for vision verification, fraud detection,
economist analysis, and graph synthesis. Knowledge Layer: Neo4j knowledge graph storing
complex relationships between entities, programs, and impact assets. Data Layer: PostgreSQL+PostGIS for transactional data, Redis for caching/sessions, MinIO/S3 for evidence
blobs. Infrastructure Layer: Docker/Kubernetes orchestration, Kafka event bus, monitoring
with Prometheus/Grafana.

Sync and Async Communication The platform employs a dual-transport strategy. Synchronous REST is used when a caller requires an immediate answer, a bounded timeout, and
an explicit error contract, with HTTP semantics and each operation described through OpenAPI. Asynchronous events distribute facts, isolate failure domains, absorb bursts, and support
independent downstream processing, with channels, operations, and message schemas described
through AsyncAPI. Neither transport is universally preferred; the choice and its compatibility
policy are recorded in the interface contract (AsyncAPI Initiative, 2023; Fielding et al., 2022;
OpenAPI Initiative, 2024).
Synchronous communication. Client and service requests use resource-oriented HTTP
through FastAPI and the gateway, which performs routing, authentication, quotas, response
aggregation, and standardized error mapping without taking ownership of domain decisions.
Asynchronous communication. Durable domain facts are published to Kafka with atleast-once delivery and idempotent consumers, while the Transactional Outbox aligns database
commits with eventual publication. Kafka transactions or exactly-once stream processing are
reserved for bounded Kafka-to-Kafka workflows where their guarantees justify the operational
cost. NATS supplies ephemeral, low-latency fan-out for chat presence, typing indicators, and
live-feed hints, but authoritative facts remain in domain stores and Kafka (Apache Software
Foundation, 2026; Synadia Communications, 2026).
Database Strategy The platform uses a polyglot persistence approach:
Relational and geospatial state. PostgreSQL is the primary relational technology for
service-owned transactional data, with isolation selected according to invariant and concurrency requirements; PostGIS provides indexed spatial relationships for geofence and proximity
decisions. Graph state. Neo4j stores governed relationship projections among organizations,
programs, tasks, participants, evidence, and impact assets, with operational procedures for
backup, upgrade, security, and monitoring. Ephemeral and cached state. Redis supports
sessions, rate limits, and caches, but durability-dependent use cases explicitly select and test
an appropriate persistence mode. Object and search state. Versioned S3-compatible storage holds large evidence objects behind service-owned metadata and policy, while OpenSearch
remains an optional derived index that must be rebuildable from authoritative sources (Neo4j,
Inc., 2026; PostGIS Project Steering Committee, 2026; PostgreSQL Global Development Group,
2026; Redis Ltd., 2026).

4 Service Domain Architecture
The MASAREN platform is organized into bounded contexts that may contain one or more
deployable workloads. Each context encapsulates a distinct business capability with explicit
data ownership, event contracts, and API surfaces. The target is not a fixed number of microservices; the target is independently understandable ownership with deployment boundaries
justified by measurable operational needs.
Bounded-Context Collaboration Map The service catalog is organized around business
capabilities rather than technical layers. Figure 4 identifies the permitted direction of highvalue business facts. Identity supplies principals and assurance; Execution supplies programs,
tasks, and assignments; Verification supplies decisions and provenance; Reputation supplies
impact assets and scores; Marketplace consumes eligibility and reputation without becoming
their owner; and Reporting builds read-optimized projections. Economic settlement references
verified outcomes but cannot modify the underlying evidence or decision.

Each arrow represents a versioned API or event contract, not permission to access another
context’s database. Cycles are resolved through orchestration, process managers, or compensating actions rather than synchronous call chains. A domain service may begin as a module in
a modular deployment and later become an independent workload when scale, risk, ownership,
or release cadence proves the need. This avoids unnecessary microservice fragmentation while
preserving clear seams for growth.
Service Integration Playbook Every integration follows the same contract template. The
owning service is authoritative for its aggregate and exposes a synchronous API only when
the caller needs an immediate decision. HTTP interfaces are described with OpenAPI, eventdriven interfaces with AsyncAPI and CloudEvents-compatible envelopes, and distributed requests propagate a standard trace context. Material facts include an event identifier, tenant,
subject, schema version, correlation identifier, causation identifier, and occurrence time. Callers
apply deadlines, retry only safe operations, attach idempotency keys to commands, and use circuit breakers for remote dependencies; consumers use a durable inbox and commit the event
identifier with the business-side effect. These conventions make the boundary understandable
to humans and tools without granting database access (AsyncAPI Initiative, 2023; Cloud Native
Computing Foundation, 2024; OpenAPI Initiative, 2024; World Wide Web Consortium, 2021).
The examples below name the normal upstream caller, downstream consumer, and concrete
transaction for each service.
API Gateway. The gateway receives HTTPS requests from the web, admin, mobile,
11

and partner channels; validates the access token with Authentication and Authorization; and
routes the request to the owning domain service. For example, POST /programs/P-42/tasks
is authenticated, tenant-scoped, rate-limited, assigned a correlation identifier, and forwarded
to Task Service; the gateway returns Task Service’s response without reproducing its business
rules.
Identity Service. Identity receives registration or profile commands from the gateway
and identity-provider claims from Authentication. It supplies stable person, service-principal,
membership, consent, and assurance references to Organization, Program, Task, Verification,
Reputation, Billing, Chat, and Reporting. For example, after a participant verifies an email
address, Identity publishes identity_verified; Task Service may then permit assignment
while Reporting updates the verified-participant projection.
Authentication Service.

Authentication integrates with external OpenID Connect

providers, Identity, the gateway, and the audit pipeline. It creates short-lived sessions and
standards-conformant JSON Web Tokens but does not decide resource-level permission. For
example, a mobile authorization-code exchange validates PKCE, maps the provider subject
to an Identity record, rotates the refresh token, records the session, and returns an audiencebound access token; higher-risk clients may sender-constrain tokens with proof of possession.
The assurance level, authenticator choice, and federation controls are selected through the
platform’s identity risk assessment (Fett et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2015; Lodderstedt et al., 2025;
National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2025; OpenID Foundation, 2023; Sakimura
et al., 2015).
Authorization Service. Authorization consumes identity, tenant membership, role, resource relationship, policy version, and assurance signals and returns an allow, deny, or step-up
decision. It is called by the gateway for coarse checks and by domain services for high-value
resource decisions. For example, Verification Service asks whether reviewer R-17 may override
case VC-900; Authorization checks the reviewer assignment and separation-of-duties policy and
returns the policy identifier with the decision.
Organization Service. Organization owns tenant institutions, memberships, delivery capacity, and accountable administrators. It consumes verified identities and supplies organization
references to Program, Billing, Partner, Reputation, and Reporting. For example, approving
organization ORG-8 publishes organization_approved, allowing Program Service to accept a
funded program and Billing Service to open the correct customer account.
Program Service. Program receives program-design commands from authorized organization users and validates sponsor, geography, indicator, budget, and policy references. It
sends outcome definitions to Workflow, Task, Verification, Opportunity, Billing, and Reporting. For example, activating a reforestation program publishes program_activated; Workflow
creates the approved process template, Task opens field work, and Reporting initializes outcome
baselines.
Task Service. Task consumes active programs, identity eligibility, organization membership, and workflow rules and owns task and assignment state. It publishes assignment and
completion facts to Workflow, Notification, Submission, Reputation projections, and Reporting. For example, accepting task T-51 uses an idempotency key, verifies capacity with Program,

12

stores the assignment, and publishes task_assigned for Notification to alert the participant.
Workflow Service. Workflow coordinates long-running program processes without owning
the underlying domain entities. It consumes events from Program, Task, Submission, Verification, Billing, and Partner services, persists process state, and issues explicit commands to
the next owner. For example, task_completed advances a workflow from field execution to
evidence review and commands Submission Service to open a submission window; a timeout
creates an escalation rather than silently skipping the step.
Verification Service. Verification owns the case state machine and combines policy,
GPS, media, fraud, model, and human-review signals. It consumes submission_finalized
and evidence-analysis events, calls Authorization for reviewer actions, and publishes signed decisions to Economist, Impact Score, Audit, Billing, Graph, and Reporting. For example, a
case with valid location and media but low model confidence moves to human review; approval
publishes verification_approved with the policy version and evidence hashes.
Evidence Service. Evidence owns evidence metadata, checksums, provenance, retention
class, access policy, and object references while binary content resides in versioned object storage.
It integrates with Submission, Media, GPS, Verification, Audit, and privacy workflows. For
example, an image upload is malware-scanned, hashed, stored under a tenant-scoped key, and
acknowledged to Submission only after the metadata and object version are durably linked.
GPS Service. GPS receives device coordinates and capture metadata through Submission,
evaluates geofence, accuracy, time, motion, and spoofing signals, and returns a signed location
assessment to Verification and Fraud Detection. For example, a capture 18 meters inside the
approved project polygon with acceptable accuracy publishes location_validated; a mocklocation signal publishes location_flagged instead.
Media Service. Media creates safe derivatives, extracts technical metadata, performs
format validation, and prepares images, audio, or video for Vision AI and authorized viewers.
It consumes Evidence object references and publishes derivative readiness or quarantine results.
For example, a submitted video is transcoded to a review format, stripped of unsafe metadata,
assigned preview thumbnails, and then released to Vision AI through a time-limited object
reference.
Audit Service. Audit receives append-only security and business decision records from
every service and exports them to immutable archival storage and the security monitoring
system. It never becomes the owner of domain state. For example, a verification override records
the actor, before and after state, justification, policy, device, trace identifier, and evidence hashes
so an auditor can reconstruct the decision without querying application logs.
Submission Service. Submission owns the participant’s draft, required evidence checklist,
declaration, and finalization state. It integrates with Task, Evidence, GPS, Media, Workflow,
Verification, and Notification. For example, finalization confirms that the assigned task is active,
all required evidence objects are durable, consent is current, and the declaration is signed before
publishing submission_finalized.
Vision AI Service. Vision AI consumes approved media derivatives and the program’s
evidence rubric, invokes a registered multimodal model, validates the structured output, and
publishes observations to Verification rather than a final business decision. For example, it may

13

report that 47 tree seedlings are visible with confidence intervals and identify blurred regions;
Verification compares this with the task requirement and other signals.
Fraud Detection Service. Fraud Detection consumes identity risk, device, timing, GPS,
media similarity, submission velocity, and prior-case signals and returns explainable risk indicators to Verification and Audit. For example, the same photograph appearing in three tenants produces duplicate_evidence_suspected with the matching hashes; Verification decides
whether review or rejection is appropriate.
Economist Agent. The Economist Agent consumes an approved verification case, outcome
methodology, market or sponsor coeﬀicients, and program budget constraints and proposes a
valuation record. The Reputation or Economic owner validates deterministic rules before an
asset or ledger movement is created. For example, an approved water-delivery outcome is
translated into a proposed quantity and value with the methodology version and calculation
inputs attached for reviewer inspection.
Graph Synthesis Agent. Graph Synthesis consumes verified entity and relationship facts,
proposes normalized graph mutations, and sends them to Graph Service through a schemavalidated command. It cannot write Neo4j directly. For example, impact_asset_created
yields a proposed relationship among participant, organization, program, location, outcome,
and methodology; Graph Service checks identifiers and applies the idempotent mutation.
AI Orchestrator. The orchestrator selects registered models and tools, enforces dataclassification and budget policy, manages timeouts and fallbacks, and records inference lineage.
It coordinates Vision, Fraud, Economist, Auditor, Planner, Broker, Recommendation, Speech,
and Compliance modules without becoming the owner of their business outcomes. For example,
a provider timeout can route an eligible request to a second approved model while preserving
the same correlation identifier and recording the fallback reason.
Auditor Agent. Auditor Agent reads a bounded evidence package from Audit, Verification,
Program, and policy stores and proposes control findings for a human auditor. For example, it
can identify that a review occurred after the service-level deadline or that a policy version was
superseded, but only Audit or Compliance owners can close the finding.
Broker Agent. Broker Agent consumes approved opportunities, participant eligibility,
reputation, geography, capacity, and declared preferences and proposes candidate matches to
Matching Service. For example, it can rank three qualified field teams for an opportunity
while exposing the factors used; Matching applies hard policy constraints and records the final
recommendation.
Planner Agent. Planner Agent consumes program objectives, resource capacity, calendars,
geography, risk, and historical throughput and proposes schedules or task decomposition to
Program and Workflow. For example, it may propose dividing a regional survey into ten tasks
with travel-eﬀicient sequencing; an authorized program manager approves the plan before tasks
are created.
Recommendation Agent. Recommendation Agent consumes consented profile, behavior,
eligibility, and opportunity projections and proposes personalized content to Marketplace or the
user experience layer. For example, it recommends an Arabic-language training program to an
eligible participant but suppresses sensitive inferences and records why the item was selected.

Speech AI Service. Speech AI consumes an authorized audio evidence derivative, performs
transcription and language detection, and returns timestamped text and confidence to Evidence
and Verification. For example, a participant’s Arabic field statement is transcribed, linked to the
original audio hash, and routed for human confirmation when confidence is below the configured
threshold.
Compliance Service. Compliance maps program, partner, privacy, evidence, and transaction facts to jurisdictional and contractual rules and raises blocking or advisory findings. For
example, a proposed cross-region evidence transfer is denied when the data classification and
tenant policy require regional residency; the denial and policy reference are sent to Audit and
Workflow.
Media Distribution Service. Media Distribution receives explicitly approved public
assets from Reporting or Program, creates channel-specific renditions, and publishes through
configured external channels. It never publishes raw evidence. For example, an approved impact
story is resized, localized, watermarked, scheduled, and posted after consent and brand checks
pass.
Impact Score Service. Impact Score consumes approved impact assets, reversals, methodology versions, and time-decay rules and owns the participant score projection. It publishes an
explainable score change to Portfolio, Matching, Graph, Notification, and Reporting. For example, an approved asset increases a participant’s environmental score; a later revocation produces
a compensating score event rather than rewriting history.
Organization Score Service. Organization Score aggregates verified program outcomes,
quality, timeliness, disputes, and reversals for an organization. It consumes Organization,
Program, Verification, and Impact Score facts and supplies explainable indicators to Marketplace, Partner, Graph, and Reporting. For example, repeated late evidence reviews reduce an
operational-quality component while leaving unrelated impact quantities unchanged.
Portfolio Service. Portfolio owns the user’s or organization’s read model of impact assets,
certificates, score explanations, and visibility settings. It consumes Reputation and Verification events and serves the web, mobile, partner export, and reporting channels. For example,
impact_asset_created adds a pending-visible asset, while certificate generation later attaches
a signed document reference.
Opportunity Service. Opportunity owns sponsor demand, eligibility, geography, capacity,
application window, and commercial terms. It consumes approved organization and program
references and publishes opportunity lifecycle events to Matching, Notification, Partner, Billing,
and Reporting. For example, publishing a coastal-cleanup opportunity triggers Matching to
evaluate qualified organizations and Notification to alert opted-in users.
Matching Service. Matching combines Opportunity constraints with Identity, Organization, Reputation, Portfolio, and consented preference projections. It stores the explainable
match result and supports acceptance, rejection, and appeal. For example, a high-scoring candidate is excluded when required regional certification is absent, while the next eligible candidate
is returned with the decisive factors.
Billing Service. Billing owns customer accounts, invoices, credits, tax context, and receivables while referencing programs, subscriptions, and verified outcomes. It integrates with

15

Organization, Program, Subscription, Payment, Verification, Partner, and Reporting. For example, an outcome-based contract creates an invoice line only after verification_approved
and the relevant contractual milestone are both present.
Payment Service. Payment isolates payment-provider APIs, tokenized payment methods,
intents, captures, refunds, webhooks, and reconciliation. It consumes payable invoices from
Billing and publishes settlement facts back to Billing, Audit, Notification, and Reporting. For
example, a provider webhook is signature-validated and deduplicated before payment_settled
closes the invoice.
Subscription Service. Subscription owns plans, entitlements, renewal state, usage allowances, trials, and cancellation timing. It integrates with Billing, Payment, Organization,
Platform, and the gateway’s entitlement checks. For example, a successful renewal publishes
subscription_renewed; Platform updates tenant limits and Billing records the next cycle.
Coin Service. Coin owns the MASAREN internal-unit ledger, issuance policy, transfer
constraints, and redemption references; it does not masquerade as legal tender or replace the
financial ledger. It consumes authorized reward or settlement commands from Economic services and publishes balances to Portfolio, Notification, Audit, and Reporting. For example, an
approved incentive creates balanced debit and credit entries tied to the verification case and
idempotency key.
Notification Service. Notification consumes user-facing events from Task, Workflow, Verification, Marketplace, Billing, Payment, Subscription, Chat, and Reputation. It applies preference, consent, urgency, localization, and quiet-hour policy before delivering through push, email,
SMS, or in-app channels. For example, task_assigned produces an Arabic push notification
and an in-app record, while delivery failure is retried without replaying the task command.
Chat Service. Chat owns conversations, membership, messages, moderation state, and
delivery receipts and uses NATS or WebSocket fan-out for live presence. It integrates with
Identity, Organization, Program, Task, Media, Notification, Compliance, and Audit. For example, a program team opens a task-linked conversation; attachments are routed through Media
and Evidence controls, and an offline recipient receives a notification.
Reporting Services. Reporting consumes governed events and snapshots from every
bounded context into versioned analytical projections. It produces operational dashboards,
sponsor reports, audit extracts, and public impact summaries without issuing domain commands. For example, a sponsor report joins program targets, approved verification cases, payments, and methodology versions and exposes drill-through links to authorized evidence.
Partner Service. Partner owns partner registration, credentials, scopes, webhook subscriptions, quotas, sandbox access, and data-sharing agreements. It integrates with Identity,
Authorization, Program, Opportunity, Billing, Audit, and the gateway. For example, a partner
subscribes to opportunity_published; Partner signs the webhook, retries with backoff, and
disables delivery after repeated failures without blocking Opportunity Service.
Ads Service. Ads owns campaign, placement, creative approval, budget, targeting constraints, and delivery measurement. It consumes only consented and policy-approved audience
segments from Reporting or Recommendation and sends billing facts to Billing. For example, a sponsor campaign targets organizations interested in a public program category without

Platform Service. Platform owns tenant configuration, feature flags, service catalog
metadata, maintenance windows, usage limits, and operational policy references. It integrates
with Subscription, Organization, gateway, deployment automation, and all services that consume configuration. For example, enabling a verification feature for one pilot tenant publishes
tenant_configuration_changed; services refresh the versioned configuration without redeployment.
Graph Service. Graph Service is the sole writer to the knowledge graph and validates
entity identifiers, relationship types, provenance, and replay safety. It consumes approved
mutations from domain events and Graph Synthesis and supplies relationship queries to Reputation, Matching, Reporting, and authorized investigators. For example, a revoked impact
asset marks the relationship as invalid from the revocation time instead of deleting historical
lineage.
Authentication compatibility adapter. If the separate Python authentication workload
on development port 8093 remains necessary, it acts only as a compatibility adapter behind
Authentication Service and may not create a second identity or token authority. For example,
a legacy client request is translated to the canonical OpenID Connect flow and the resulting
session is still owned and audited by Authentication Service.
Integration acceptance. Each service team must provide an OpenAPI or event schema,

idempotency behavior, timeout and retry policy, data classification, observability fields, compatibility tests, and a failure-mode test. A service is not considered integrated merely because
another service can call it in a development environment.

Failure and replay behavior. If Vision AI is unavailable, AI Orchestrator uses an approved fallback or routes the case to manual review. If Kafka is unavailable after a domain commit, the outbox retains the publication intent until the relay succeeds. If a consumer crashes

and replay without changing the authoritative Program, Task, Submission, Verification, Asset,
or Ledger records.

Duplicate Table Resolution A critical challenge of merging 30+ independent services is that

is a monkey-patch on Table.__new__ that deduplicates table registrations:

_orig_table_new = _Table.__new__
def _patched_table_new(cls, *args, **kwargs):
metadata = kwargs.get("metadata") or \
(args[1] if len(args) >= 2 else None)
name = kwargs.get("name") or \
(args[0] if args else None)
schema = kwargs.get("schema")
if metadata is not None and name is not None:
key = (schema, name) if schema else name
if key in metadata.tables:
return metadata.tables[key]
return _orig_table_new(cls, *args, **kwargs)
_Table.__new__ = staticmethod(_patched_table_new)

Authentication Flow The authentication journey begins when the user opens the sign-in
route and is redirected to the configured OpenID Connect authorization endpoint. After the

Transactional Outbox Pattern To ensure atomicity between an aggregate change and the
intent to publish its event, a service writes both records in one local database transaction. A
separate relay claims unpublished rows, publishes with idempotent producer settings, records
the broker result, and retries with bounded backoff. Consumers still assume duplicates because
a crash can occur between publication and marking an outbox row as sent.

Event Processing Pipeline A domain command first validates invariants and changes the
aggregate inside a local PostgreSQL transaction. That same transaction inserts the corresponding outbox record, ensuring that committed state always retains its publication intent. A relay
then claims pending rows, serializes the governed event envelope, publishes to the correct Kafka
topic, and records the broker acknowledgement; failures leave the row available for bounded
retry and operational escalation. Downstream consumers assume at-least-once delivery, insert
the event identifier into a durable inbox, apply the business-side effect in the same transaction,
and advance their consumer position only after commit. This sequence prevents a lost event
from being mistaken for a successful command and prevents a redelivery from applying the
same effect twice (Apache Software Foundation, 2026; PostgreSQL Global Development Group,
2026).

11 Testing Strategy
The MASAREN platform uses risk-based, layered verification. Code coverage is monitored as a
diagnostic signal, not used as the primary quality target: a high percentage cannot demonstrate
correct authorization, safe migrations, consumer idempotency, recovery, or business outcomes.
Release gates prioritize critical-journey behavior, changed-code coverage, mutation or property testing for high-risk domain rules, contract compatibility, security controls, performance
budgets, and recoverability.
Testing Pyramid Testing is organized as a connected body of evidence rather than a stack
of independent checkboxes. Fast unit and property tests protect domain invariants, while integration tests exercise real PostgreSQL, Kafka, Redis, object-storage, and provider adapters and
prove the outbox and inbox behavior end to end. Contract suites validate HTTP operations
against OpenAPI and event channels against AsyncAPI, including backward compatibility and
consumer-driven examples. Browser and mobile journey tests cover the participant and reviewer workflows, resilience exercises inject dependency timeouts, broker or database failover,
retry storms, zone loss, restoration, replay, and rollback, and security and privacy suites verify
tenant isolation, authorization, abuse controls, secret handling, audit completeness, retention,
export, deletion, and consent transitions (AsyncAPI Initiative, 2023; OpenAPI Initiative, 2024;
OWASP Foundation, 2023, 2024, 2025a).

12 Security Architecture
Authentication and Authorization The platform implements defense in depth and treats
identity as a continuously evaluated control plane. Network location does not confer trust.
Human users authenticate through an OpenID Connect provider with phishing-resistant multifactor authentication required for administrators and reviewers. Services authenticate through
short-lived workload identities and mutually authenticated transport, not shared API keys
copied into environment files. Authorization combines tenant membership, role, resource relationship, data sensitivity, geography, assurance level, and explicit policy; coarse RBAC remains
useful but is insuﬀicient on its own for evidence, financial, and administrative decisions. The
control baseline combines NIST zero-trust and digital-identity guidance with OWASP ASVS
for web applications, the API Security Top 10 for service boundaries, and MASVS for mobilespecific threats (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2020, 2025; OWASP Foundation, 2023, 2024, 2025a).
User authentication and sessions. Browser and mobile clients use the authorizationcode flow with PKCE. Access tokens are JSON Web Tokens that are audience-bound and
short lived; refresh tokens rotate, are sender-constrained where supported, and are revoked
on reuse, risk, or account-state change. Authorization policy. Roles such as participant,
organization administrator, reviewer, finance operator, auditor, and platform administrator
are scoped to tenants and resources, while sensitive actions require step-up authentication,
separation of duties, and policy-decision logging. Workload identity. Services receive shortlived credentials from the runtime identity plane, and mutual TLS plus policy identify both caller
and destination so no permanent shared service credential is embedded in an image or repository.
Abuse and boundary controls. The edge applies origin checks, schema validation, requestsize limits, quotas, bot and credential-stuﬀing controls, and risk-based rate limits keyed by
identity, tenant, route, and network attributes. Secure data access. Parameterized queries,
least-privilege database roles, tenant scoping, row-level controls where appropriate, and serviceowned schemas reduce injection and lateral-access risk (Fett et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2015;
Lodderstedt et al., 2025; OpenID Foundation, 2023; Sakimura et al., 2015).

enforcement, AI evaluation, and contract compatibility.
The roadmap is organized as a sequence of evidence-based phases rather than a collection of disconnected feature requests. Foundation phase. Establish tenant and workload
identity, the contract registry, data classification, retention enforcement, trace propagation,
SLO dashboards, cost allocation, deployment policy, and tested backups. Exit requires one
production-like environment and evidence for every Tier-1 control.
Verification-market fit phase. Complete the evidence-to-asset journey, reviewer tooling,
appeals, sponsor reporting, partner sandbox, mobile offline conflict handling, and governed
reputation methodology. Exit requires pilot customers to complete representative journeys
with measured trust, latency, cost, and reviewer-effort outcomes.
Scale phase. Introduce search, additional projections, data partitioning, regional recovery,
and selective service extraction only where measurements demonstrate need. Event sourcing
and saga orchestration are adopted per workflow when audit reconstruction or distributed compensation requires them; they are not platform-wide goals.
Ecosystem phase. Publish stable partner APIs, consent-aware data-sharing products, certification evidence, developer tooling, and commercial service tiers. Expansion to additional
regions or AI providers occurs only after legal, privacy, operational, and unit-economics review.
